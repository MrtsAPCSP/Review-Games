Essential knowledge, for the record

Etruscan and Roman art is grounded in civic ideals and polytheism. Artists accumulated and adapted Greek forms — eclecticism and historicism over invention from scratch.

Republic vs. Empire

Republic (509–27 BCE): veristic busts, wrinkles as gravitas. Empire (27 BCE–410 CE): idealized Greek-derived bodies as propaganda.

The Alae

wings off the atrium — the Etruscan story

Before Rome, there was Etruria. Lucius kept several old family pieces here — study them before you search.

Sarcophagus of the Spouses terracotta, c. 520 BCE

Husband and wife reclining as equal partners — unusually high status for a woman in this period.

Apollo of Veii terracotta, c. 510–500 BCE

A striding rooftop temple statue; Greek Archaic smile, Etruscan material and motion.

Tomb of the Triclinium fresco, c. 470 BCE

A painted banquet in the afterlife — pleasure, not solemn judgment.

Capitoline Wolf bronze, c. 500–480 BCE

Lost-wax cast; the twins were added over a thousand years later.

The Capitol

temple hill — the gods Lucius prayed to

The Capitoline Hill's great temple housed the Capitoline Triad — Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva — in a triple-cella design borrowed straight from Etruscan temple architecture. Roman religion itself is a case study in reception: most Roman gods are Greek gods wearing Latin names.

Notice the pattern from the Peristyle room repeating here: Greek content, Roman relabeling. Augustus's cupid (Peristyle room) claiming descent from Venus only makes sense once you know Venus is Rome's version of Aphrodite.
