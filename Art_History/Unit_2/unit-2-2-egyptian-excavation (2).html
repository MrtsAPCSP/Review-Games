Egypt's richest, most powerful era. Ahmose I reunites the kingdom in 1550 BCE, Hatshepsut rules as one of the most successful female pharaohs, and burials move from pyramids to hidden tombs cut into the Valley of the Kings.

Temple of Amun-Re

Karnak · c. 1550–1250 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Cut sandstone & mud brick; hypostyle hall of 134 columns like a "forest of columns."
  • Function: State temple to Amun-Re, entered through monumental pylons.
  • Content: Clerestory windows drop light into the dense colonnade.
  • Context: Expanded by generations of pharaohs to show continuous devotion.

Mortuary Temple of Hatshepsut

Deir el-Bahri · c. 1473–1458 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Axial plan — an elongated ground plan set into the cliffs.
  • Function: Funerary temple honoring Hatshepsut after death.
  • Content: Terraced colonnades echo the mountain behind them.
  • Context: Built by one of the few female pharaohs to rule in her own right.

Innermost Coffin of Tutankhamen

Valley of the Kings · c. 1323 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Solid gold with enamel & semiprecious stone inlay, 6'1" long.
  • Function: Sarcophagus housing the king's mummified body and Ka.
  • Content: Crook and flail crossed on the chest — symbols of kingship.
  • Context: Found intact by Howard Carter in 1922, nested inside two more coffins.

Last Judgment of Hunefer

Book of the Dead · c. 1275 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Painted papyrus scroll, read left to right.
  • Function: Guides the deceased through judgment in the underworld.
  • Content: Anubis weighs Hunefer's heart against the feather of Ma'at while Ammit waits to devour failures.
  • Context: This exact scene inspired the Trial waiting for you below.

Someone tried to erase this from the record.

Akhenaton, Nefertiti, and Three Daughters

Amarna Period · c. 1353–1335 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Limestone sunken relief — outlines cut deep so figures seem to project forward.
  • Function: Domestic devotional scene, not a formal state portrait.
  • Content: The family basks under Aton, the sun-disk — Egypt's first experiment with a single god.
  • Context: Bodies are soft, curving, and unidealized — the one moment Egyptian art broke its own rules.

Thinner layer, fewer grave goods — but a turning point in the record. Pharaoh Mentuhotep II reunites Upper and Lower Egypt after civil war, royal burials shift north near Memphis, and irrigation canals start carrying Nile water directly to the fields.

Reunification & Irrigation

field summary
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Mentuhotep II reunites Egypt around 2040 BCE, ending the First Intermediate Period.
  • Royal tombs relocate north, closer to Memphis.
  • New irrigation systems pull Nile water to crops, boosting food security.
  • Sets the stage for the wealth and expansion of the New Kingdom above.

The Age of Pyramids. Ra the sun god rises to prominence, the pyramid's shape echoes his sacred benben stone, and the Giza plateau becomes the grandest necropolis ever built. By Dynasty 7–8 the state weakens and the layer above thins into famine.

Seated Scribe

Saqqara · c. 2500 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Painted limestone, inlaid rock-crystal eyes, 1'9" high.
  • Function: A Ka statue — a home for the soul if the mummy failed.
  • Content: Alert, individualized face; unusually relaxed pose for Egyptian art.
  • Context: Scribes held real social status — literacy was power.

Great Pyramids & Great Sphinx

Giza · c. 2550–2490 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Cut limestone, originally faced in white limestone with a gleaming capstone.
  • Function: Tombs for Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure; the Sphinx guards the necropolis.
  • Content: Pyramid shape recalls the benben, the sacred mound of the sun cult.
  • Context: Evolved from the mastaba through the step pyramid to this final form.

King Menkaure & his Queen

Gizeh · c. 2490–2472 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Graywacke, 4'6.5" high, both figures carved from one block.
  • Function: Idealized funerary portrait for the afterlife.
  • Content: Rigid, forward-facing "Egyptian stance"; queen's embrace signals unity, not affection.
  • Context: The permanent, unchanging pose was meant to last for eternity.

Bedrock. This is where the Egyptian visual language is born — hierarchy of scale, composite pose, and hieroglyphic writing all appear together, fully formed, on a single slate palette.

Palette of King Narmer

Hierakonpolis · c. 3000–2920 BCE
tap to flip

Field notes

  • Form: Slate, 2'1" high, carved on both sides.
  • Function: Ceremonial object commemorating the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt.
  • Content: Narmer in the smiting pose wears the White Crown on one side, the Red Crown on the other.
  • Context: Sets the "Egyptian stance" — composite frontal/profile pose — that lasts 3,000 years.
